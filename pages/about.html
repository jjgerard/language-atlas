What this is, and what it can’t do yet

Four maps of language policy, covering every country in the world — most places now carry something, most of them thinly, and the colour shows which.

It grew out of two catalogues, one on school language support and one on language disorder provision, so that the same country could be compared across subjects rather than across websites. Both are held here now, alongside maps for foreign languages and for the languages already spoken where the school stands.

Where the project is

336 places on each map, 1,344 slots in all. About four in five carry a record — but a record can be one sourced sentence, and across every field of every entry roughly a third are filled. Africa, the Americas, Europe and Oceania have a record everywhere on the three older maps; Asia does not, and China’s provinces have deliberately not been started.

Much of the map is grey or pale, and that is the honest picture rather than a gap in effort. A catalogue showing only the countries someone has already researched quietly implies its scope is “wherever we got to”, and a researcher from an undocumented country has no way to know they are the missing piece.

Reading the map

Documented. The colour says how much: the ramp runs from a fifth of this map’s fields filled to nearly all. It measures what we know, not how good the policy is — a pale country may have an excellent system nobody has written down here yet.
Looked, found nothing. Someone searched and recorded finding nothing, and where they looked. Not the same as nobody having tried.
Nothing recorded. Nobody has documented this place for this subject yet.
Follows its country. A region with nothing of its own, showing what its country records instead.

A sub-national unit exists because it differs from its country in some subject — the US states are separate because their school-language policy differs — so on a map where it has nothing of its own it follows the national entry rather than reading as a hole in it.

Why Indigenous and regional languages have their own map

They used to sit on the foreign-languages map, whose own description covers languages taught besides the language of the school. In Nunavut, Inuktut is the language of the school. Contributors had started writing warnings about the heading above them — one entry read “Inuktut is an official language, not minority provision”. When an entry argues with its own label, the label is wrong.

The name is a compromise. Counted across the sources here, there is no shared term: Canada’s say Indigenous, Africa’s say national languages, India’s say linguistic minorities — for the same kind of language, and in Africa’s case usually for the majority one. Every available term is a regional loan. Each entry records the word its own sources use in its Local term field: the atlas names the category, the sources keep theirs.

What the map records is structural rather than evaluative — whether school is taught in a language, teaches it as a subject, recognises it without an entitlement, teaches about a community rather than its language, or none of these.

Where the language data comes from

Languages are named with their family, basic typology and a link to their record in the World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS Online, ed. Dryer & Haspelmath), under CC BY 4.0. WALS describes varieties: there is no single record for “Quechua” or “Inuktitut”, but several. Where a language has no record it is still named, without a link — a missing link means WALS has no entry, not that the language is unimportant.

Each national entry also says how many languages the country actually has, counted from Glottolog (CC BY 4.0): Papua New Guinea 899, Nigeria 589, India 518. What the entry lists is what the school system engages with, and the distance between the two numbers is usually the point. The count is national only — India’s 518 is not Kerala’s number.

How entries are sourced

Every entry carries a confidence rating — official document, researcher-verified, secondary source, or unverified submission — independent of how much detail it has. A one-line, high-confidence entry is worth more than a long unsourced one. Each also carries a last verified date, and a citation that cannot be resolved to a real source stays as plain text rather than being given a plausible-looking link.

Known sticking points

  • Policy versus practice. What a system must provide and what a child receives can be far apart. Contributors are asked to say which they are describing.
  • The category boundary moves. Some systems have no “DLD” category at all, only something broader; some have no designation for a pupil learning the language of instruction. Any cross-system count compares different objects, which is why the terminology and criteria fields exist.
  • Survey figures are perceptions. Several fields come from the COST Action IS1406 practitioner survey, where respondents described one child of their own choosing in 2017 — sampled cases, never a budget or a legal position.
  • Assessments are named and linked, never reproduced. No norms, items or cut-off tables.
  • Sources are mostly not in English. Every entry has a source-language note.

What this is not

A catalogue of systems — not of children, and not of clinical practice. Nothing here should be used to decide whether a particular child has a language disorder or what support they should receive, and contributors are asked not to submit anything about identifiable individuals.

It is also not a ranking. There is no score, no index and no league table, and there is not going to be one: a single number would imply a comparison the underlying entries cannot support. That is the main thing separating this from MIPEX, whose strength is exactly the comparable index a catalogue of criteria cannot honestly produce. Eurydice covers roughly 40 European systems in real depth and is the best source there is for them; for language disorders there is no maintained comparative database at all, only fixed snapshots. All of them stop at Europe and the OECD.

Contributing

If you know how a system works for a place that is still a stub — or can add one field to an existing entry — it is worth adding. Partial is fine, and “there is no policy on this” is a real answer. Everything is reviewed before publishing, never automatically.

Open any place on a map and the end of its entry has the way in, or use the form directly. A place can be built one field at a time by different people, and each contribution is credited on it.

Who built this

Built by Juliana Gerard, a lecturer in Linguistics at Ulster University. The application code was written with Claude Code (Anthropic), which was also used to locate policy documents, extract what each source establishes, and draft entries; the human work is setting the schema, directing the research, and reviewing what gets published.

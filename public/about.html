About this resource

World maps about language policy in various domains.

Each map focuses on one general domain:

  • Newcomers and second-language learners asks what a school does for a child who arrives without its language.
  • Language disorder support asks what happens when a child’s own language does not develop as expected.
  • Foreign languages asks which languages are taught besides the school’s own.
  • Indigenous and regional languages asks what school does with the languages already spoken where it stands.

The Patterns page aggregates the data to explore trends within and across the different pages. Explore, sort, and download the sources from the Sources page.

Reading the map

Documented. The colour says how much: the ramp runs from a fifth of this map’s questions answered to all of them. It measures what we know, not how good the policy is — a pale country may have an excellent system nobody has written down here yet.
Follows its country. A region with nothing of its own, showing what its country records. It counts as covered, because the national record is what applies there.
Looked, found nothing. Someone searched and recorded finding nothing, and where they looked. Not the same as nobody having tried.
Nothing recorded. Nobody has documented this place for this subject yet.
Not applicable. The question has no meaning for this place — Glottolog counts languages per country, so a state has no count of its own. Left out of that entry’s total rather than counted against it.

A sub-national unit exists because it differs from its country in some subject — the US states are separate because their school-language policy differs. It does not follow that they differ on every subject, and the colour now says so: a place is shaded by the questions that have an answer for it, whether that answer is its own or its country’s.

Every US state, for instance, sets its own definition of a language disorder and its own criteria for identifying one, and those are recorded state by state. Who pays, what the entitlement is and when support ends are settled once, federally, and there is no state-level version to record. An entry marks which of its fields are its own and which it inherits, so you can always see which is which — but a state is not a gap in the map for questions its country has already answered.

What already exists

A good deal. What each one is — a scored index, a written profile, a one-off study — is worth knowing alongside what it covers.

  • MIPEX — scored index, interactive, 56 countries. Migrant integration across eight strands, education among them, with charts and country profiles you can compare directly. The closest model for how this site presents itself.
  • Eurydice — browsable descriptions plus comparative reports, roughly 40 European systems. Its Key data on teaching languages at school volumes carry comparative figures in real depth, and it is the best source there is for the countries it covers.
  • UNESCO PEER — written profiles, near-global. One page per country on inclusion and language of instruction, with the widest coverage of anything listed here. It is the most heavily cited source on this site.
  • SIRIUS Migrant Education Policy Index (2025) — report, Europe. Assesses inclusive policy for migrant learners.
  • COST Action IS1406 — practitioner survey, European systems. The baseline for language disorders, alongside the funding analysis that came out of it. Both are studies rather than maintained databases, and several fields here draw on them directly.
  • Glottolog, WALS, Ethnologue — language inventories, global. They record which languages exist and how they are structured. This site uses the first two for the language records on the Indigenous map.

What this site adds is the grain and the reach: a sourced field for the criterion that gains or loses a designation, who pays, what happens to the home language, and which document says so, for every country rather than a region.

On Indigenous language terminology

The name is a compromise: the sources share no term — Canada’s say Indigenous, Africa’s national languages, India’s linguistic minorities — so each entry records the word its own sources use in its Local term field. What the map records is structural, not evaluative: whether school is taught in a language, teaches it as a subject, recognises it without an entitlement, teaches about a community rather than its language, or none of these.

Where the language data comes from

Languages are named with their family, basic typology and a link to WALS (WALS Online, ed. Dryer & Haspelmath, CC BY 4.0). WALS describes varieties, so there is no single record for “Quechua” or “Inuktitut”; a language with no record is still named, without a link.

Each national entry also says how many languages the country has, counted from Glottolog (CC BY 4.0): Papua New Guinea 899, Nigeria 589, India 518. What the entry lists is what the school system engages with, and the distance between the two numbers is usually the point. The count is national only.

Disclaimer

This is a catalogue of systems — not of children, and not of clinical practice. Nothing here should be used to decide whether a particular child has a language disorder, or what support they should receive. Contributors are asked not to submit anything about identifiable individuals.

It is not a ranking. There is no score, no index and no league table, and there is not going to be one: a single number would imply a comparison the underlying entries cannot support.

It records what a system says, not what a child gets. Policy and practice can be far apart, and contributors are asked to say which they are describing. Some figures come from the COST Action IS1406 practitioner survey, where respondents described one child of their own choosing in 2017 — sampled cases, never a budget or a legal position.

Counting across systems compares different objects. Some have no “DLD” category at all, only something broader; some have no designation for a pupil learning the language of instruction. That is why the terminology and criteria fields exist, and why any total here should be read with them.

Assessments are named and linked, never reproduced — no norms, items or cut-off tables. Most sources are not in English, and every entry carries a source-language note.

Contributing

If you know how a system works for a place that is still a stub — or can add one field to an existing entry — it is worth adding. Partial is fine, and “there is no policy on this” is a real answer. Everything is reviewed before publishing, never automatically.

Open any place on a map and the end of its entry has the way in, or use the form directly. A place can be built one field at a time by different people, and each contribution is credited on it.

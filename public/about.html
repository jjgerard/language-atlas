What this is, and what it can't do yet

One map per subject, covering every country in the world — most places now carry something, most of them thinly, and the map is coloured to show which.

It grew out of two separate catalogues — one on school language support, one on language disorder provision — so that the same country could be compared across subjects rather than across websites. Both have since been folded in and are held here, alongside a third map on foreign languages in school. This is now the one place any of it is kept.

Everything — the map, the entries, the form — is generated from one description of what each subject records, so a new subject is added by describing it rather than by building another site. That is what let two separate catalogues become three maps without either of them being rewritten.

Why every country is listed

A catalogue that shows only the countries someone has already researched quietly implies its scope is "wherever we got to". A researcher from an undocumented country has no way to know they are the missing piece. Listing everywhere from the start — with the gaps visible — makes the real scope of the project legible, and turns browsing into an invitation rather than a dead end.

Grey still means nothing is recorded, and there is still plenty of it.

Where the project actually is

There are four maps of 336 places each, so 1,344 slots in all. About four in five now carry a record. That sounds better than it is, because a record can be a single sourced sentence: across every field of every entry, roughly 28% are filled. Africa, the Americas, Europe and Oceania have a record everywhere on the three older maps; Asia does not, and China’s provinces have deliberately not been started.

That gap between something is here and this is complete used to be invisible, because the map coloured a place by its world region and a country with one sourced sentence looked exactly like a country with eleven. Colour is now the share of that map’s fields which have content, so the thinness is on the face of the map rather than buried in it. The work in progress is filling the best-covered fields first — a field already past 60% is one pass from being comparable across every system, where a field at 3% would still be unusable after the same effort.

What already exists

Plenty, for part of the picture. It is worth being precise about which part, because this project only earns its place where those stop.

  • MIPEX scores migrant integration policy across 56 countries, with education as one of eight strands. It is the closest model for how this site presents itself, and the reason there is no score here: MIPEX's strength is a single comparable index, which is exactly what a catalogue of criteria and entitlements cannot honestly produce.
  • Eurydice maps the integration of migrant students and the teaching of languages across roughly 40 European education systems, in genuine depth, and is the best source there is for the countries it covers.
  • The SIRIUS Migrant Education Policy Index (2025) is newer and narrower, assessing inclusive policy for migrant learners in Europe.
  • For language disorders there is no maintained comparative database at all — only studies. The COST Action IS1406 practitioner survey and the funding analysis that came out of it are the baseline, and both are fixed snapshots rather than something anyone updates.

Two things follow. All of these stop at Europe and the OECD, so for most of the world there is no comparative resource in either subject. And where they do cover a country, they report at a coarser grain than the question usually needs: whether support exists, rather than the exact criterion for gaining or losing it, who pays, what happens to the home language, and which document says so. This site is aimed at that second gap as much as the first, which is why an entry is a set of sourced fields rather than a rating.

Why Indigenous and regional languages have their own map

They used to sit in a field called “Regional and minority languages” on the foreign-languages map — whose own description says it covers languages taught besides the language of the school. In Nunavut, Inuktut is the language of the school. Contributors had started writing warnings about the heading above them: one entry read “Inuktut is an official language, not minority provision — do not read this as a minority field”. When an entry has to argue with its own label, the label is wrong.

The name is a compromise and it is worth saying why. Counted across the sources this atlas has retrieved, there is no globally shared term: Canada’s say Indigenous, Africa’s say national languages, India’s say linguistic minorities — for the same kind of language, and in Africa’s case usually for the majority one. Every available term is a regional loan. This one was chosen for legibility, and each entry records the word its own sources use in its Local term field. The atlas names the category; the sources keep theirs.

What the map records is structural and quotable rather than evaluative: whether school is taught in the language, teaches it as a subject, recognises it without an entitlement, teaches about the community rather than the language, or does none of these. There are no scores here, as everywhere else in this atlas.

Reading the map

Documented. Something is on record for this place, and the colour says how much: the ramp runs from a fifth of this map’s fields filled to nearly all of them. It measures what we know, not how good the policy is — a pale country may have an excellent system nobody has written down here yet.
Looked, found nothing. Someone searched and recorded that they found nothing, and where they looked. This is not the same as nobody having tried.
Nothing recorded. Nobody has documented this place for this subject yet.
Follows its country. A region with nothing of its own for this subject, showing what its country records instead.

Regions are split as finely as the evidence needs. A sub-national unit exists because it differs from its country in some subject — the US states are separate because their school-language policy differs — so on a map where it has nothing of its own it follows the national entry rather than reading as a hole in it. Its page says which fields are inherited and which are its own.

Three tiers of coverage

  • Where a published baseline exists. For language disorders, the 35 national vignettes in Law, McKean, Murphy & Thordardottir (2019), out of COST Action IS1406. For school language support, Eurydice and national ministry documents. The work here is extraction, updating to the current position, and adding what those sources do not cover.
  • Federal and decentralised systems. The US, Canada, Australia, Germany, Switzerland, Spain, India and others have no single national answer: eligibility, funding and workforce rules are set by state, province, Land or region. These are documented at that level rather than flattened into one national row.
  • Everywhere else. No comparative source exists, and in many systems there is no recognised speech and language therapy profession at all — which is itself the entry. Only people who work in those systems can fill this in.

How entries are sourced and trusted

Every entry carries a confidence rating — official document, researcher-verified, secondary source, or unverified submission — independent of how much detail it has. A one-line, high-confidence entry is worth more than a long unsourced one, and the point is to show both honestly rather than making everything look equally authoritative. Content status (stub, partial, complete) is a separate axis: an entry can be published and still be a stub.

Every entry also carries a last verified date. An old entry is not silently wrong, but it is not silently right either. Where a claim traces to a document, the citation in the text links to it; a citation that cannot be resolved to a real source stays as plain text rather than being given a plausible-looking link.

Known sticking points

These are stated openly so contributors know what they are walking into.

  • Policy versus practice. What a system is obliged to provide and what a child actually receives can be very far apart, and both are worth recording. A single entry can be accurate about the law and misleading about reality; contributors are asked to say which they are describing.
  • The category boundary moves. Some systems have no "DLD" category at all, only a broader class such as speech, language and communication needs. Some have no separate designation for a pupil learning the language of instruction. Any cross-system count is comparing different objects, which is why the terminology and criteria fields exist.
  • Survey figures are perceptions, not budgets. Several fields are computed from the COST Action IS1406 practitioner survey, where respondents described one child of their own choosing in 2017. Those are sampled cases and professional perception, never a national budget or a legal position.
  • Assessment tools are commercial. Naming which battery a service uses is fine; reproducing norms, items or cut-off tables is not. This project links and names, and will not host assessment content.
  • Sources are mostly not in English. Every entry has a source-language note, and anyone summarising from another language is asked to say so rather than implying a direct quotation.

What this is not

This is a catalogue of systems — not of children, and not of clinical practice. Nothing here should be used to decide whether an individual child has a language disorder, or what support any particular child should receive. Contributors are asked not to submit anything about identifiable individuals.

It is also not a ranking. There is no score, no index and no league table, and there is not going to be one: a single number would imply a comparison the underlying entries cannot support.

Contributing

If you know how a system works for a place that is still a stub — or can add even one field to an existing entry — it is worth adding. Partial is fine, and "there is no policy on this" is a real answer. Everything is reviewed before publishing, never published automatically.

Open any place on a map and the end of its entry has the way in: I know this where nothing has been recorded yet, add what I know where something has, and a way to send a correction on an entry that is already there. The form can also be opened directly, for a place the map does not split yet.

An entry does not have to be finished to be worth sending. A place can be built one field at a time by different people, and each contribution is credited on it.

This project and its content were built by Juliana Gerard, a lecturer in Linguistics at Ulster University. The application code was written with Claude Code (Anthropic), which was also used to locate policy documents and research literature, extract what each source actually establishes, and draft entries; the human work is setting the schema, directing the research, and reviewing what gets published.
